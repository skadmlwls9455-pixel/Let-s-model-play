The umbrella dealt with sunlight before it dealt with rain. The oldest image usually cited is carved on a victory stele honoring King Sargon of Akkad, from around 2310 BCE, and in Egyptian art fan-shaped canopies appear from the Fifth Dynasty, around 2450 BCE. In hot regions the umbrella was a tool for cooling the body and an object for shielding people of high rank.

The English word umbrella comes from the Latin umbra, meaning shade or shadow. The earliest usage in this sense recorded by the Oxford English Dictionary is from 1611. In its name, then, a trace remains of the time when it was a sunshade.

The umbrella that keeps off rain developed in China. Oiled-paper umbrellas, in which paper was soaked with wax and lacquer so that water would not seep in, are an example, and a folding umbrella is said to be recorded in the Book of Han as dating from 21 CE. In Europe, even in the mid-18th century a man who used an umbrella was mocked, and Jonas Hanway of London is said to have carried one steadily from around 1750. The story that Samuel Fox made the steel-ribbed umbrella in 1852 is common, but there is a record that metal ribs were sold in London in the 1780s, so it is not clear who was first.

The pocket-sized folding umbrella is known to have begun in Europe in 1710, when Jean Marius of Paris brought out a light folding one, and the pocket type of today is credited to the Hungarian Balog brothers and the German Hans Haupt in the 1920s.

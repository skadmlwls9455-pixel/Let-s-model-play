A Möbius strip is made by giving a paper strip a single half twist and joining its two ends. Made this way, it has no separate front and back, only one surface. Its edge is likewise not two lines but a single connected line. In mathematics it is regarded as the simplest of the surfaces whose orientation cannot be consistently determined, that is, "non-orientable surfaces." An asymmetric figure carried once around the strip comes back as its mirror image.

The strip was discovered independently in 1858 by the German mathematicians Johann Listing and August Möbius. Only Möbius's name remained attached to it. This means that this was when it was treated as a mathematical object, and it has also been pointed out that a similar image appears in a Roman mosaic of the 3rd century.

Cutting along the center line does not produce two pieces. It produces one long strip with four half twists. This strip is no longer a Möbius strip but is topologically the same as an ordinary cylinder. Cutting this strip down the middle again produces two interlinked strips. Cutting along a line at one third of the width produces, interlocked, one thin, long Möbius strip at the center and one strip twisted by two half turns.

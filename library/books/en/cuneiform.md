Among the very early written materials of southern Mesopotamia are clay tablets recording grain and quantities. A tablet of about 3100 to 2900 BCE held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art is also interpreted as a record concerning the distribution of grain. The early writing of this region was used mainly to keep economic information.

Not all signs were wedge-shaped from the beginning. Proto-cuneiform had pictorial signs drawn with a pointed tool and quantity marks made by pressing in round impressions. Later, a method developed in which the tip of a reed stylus was pressed into the clay to make wedge-shaped strokes. The name cuneiform comes from the look of those strokes.

Early records did not write verbs and grammatical elements fully, as sentences do today, so in some cases it is hard to fix their exact meaning. Later, as the writing system came to express language in more detail, it was also used to write poetry.

"A ledger that survives before poetry" summarizes the character of the early records handed down from this region. Whether people told stories or sang before records survived cannot be judged from the use of these tablets alone.

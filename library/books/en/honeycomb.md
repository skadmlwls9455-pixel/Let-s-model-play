When a plane is filled without gaps by cells of equal size, the shape with the shortest total length of cell perimeter is the regular hexagon. This idea is called the "honeycomb conjecture." The oldest mention on record is in Varro's writing from 36 BCE, and it is commonly said to have been stated by the mathematician Pappus around the 4th century. However, it was rigorously proved only much later. Thomas Hales proved it in 1999, and the paper was published in a journal in 2001.

A shorter perimeter means less wax goes into the walls. It is known that honeybees must eat about 3.8 kilograms of honey to produce 450 grams of wax (the figures differ from source to source), so it is natural to explain that a shape saving material benefits the bees.

Whether bees calculate in order to build hexagons is a different matter. D'Arcy Wentworth Thompson saw the hexagons as the result of cells pushing against each other and joining, like soap bubbles. As grounds for this he cited the fact that queen cells, which are built one at a time separately, are irregular. On the other hand, to the hypothesis that the wax melts and flows once like a liquid and so takes its shape, a paper denying it appeared in 2013. One ground mentioned is that the temperature of the wax during building is 33.6 to 37.6 degrees, which falls short of the 40 degrees at which it is considered a liquid. Which explanation is right has not yet been settled.

The cells of a honeycomb are not horizontal; the open side is tilted upward by about 13 degrees. A 2020 study held that this tilt does not serve to keep the honey from running out, and instead offered the interpretation that part of the weight of the contents of a cell is carried onto the central wall, allowing the honeycomb to bear more weight.

In a candle flame, the place that looks brightest to the eye is not the hottest. The part that glows yellow is where oxygen is short and the fuel burns less completely, at about 1200 degrees. The pale blue outer layer that thinly wraps it is almost invisible, but it is where complete combustion takes place, and at about 1400 degrees it is the hottest. The inner part just below the wick has little oxygen and is the coldest, at around 600 degrees. These temperatures are rough values given in the literature.

Because a candle shrinks at a steady rate, it was also used as a clock. If marks are drawn at regular intervals on a candle of even thickness, each mark becomes a fixed length of time. The oldest mention is said to be a Chinese poem of the year 520 CE, in which a 12-inch candle was marked at every inch and one section was counted as 20 minutes. Japan also used similar candles until the early 10th century. The origin is not known. In 1206, Al-Jazari described a device in which a weight pushed the candle up by as much as it had shrunk, showing the time on a dial. The story that Alfred the Great made candle clocks rests on a single record by Asser, who was at his court, so it is hard to believe as it stands.

The materials of candles differed according to price. Candles made of beef or mutton fat were cheap but gave off smoke and a smell, while beeswax candles burned cleanly but were expensive and were used mainly in churches and wealthy homes. In the mid-1850s, James Young extracted paraffin from coal and oil shale, opening the way to making cheap candles.

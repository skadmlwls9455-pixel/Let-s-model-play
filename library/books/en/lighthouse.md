A lighthouse's light is distinguished by its color and the way it flashes. This is called the light characteristic, and it is recorded in nautical charts and light lists. A light that stays on continuously is called a fixed light; one whose light is shorter than its darkness is a flashing light; one whose darkness is so short that it appears to go out briefly is an occulting light; and one whose light and darkness are equal is an isophase light. By international recommendation, a flash lasts less than 2 seconds in each occurrence, and the darkness is at least 3 times as long.

There are also patterns that repeat several flashes as a group. Fl(2) 10s on a chart means that a group of two flashes repeats every 10 seconds. When no color is given, the light is generally white. Neighboring lighthouses sometimes have towers painted with different patterns so that they are not confused even in daytime.

Sending the light far is the job of the lens. A Fresnel lens divides the lens into concentric ring segments, so it uses much less glass, and the prisms on the outside gather even obliquely directed light and add it to the beam. A similar stepped idea was first proposed by Buffon in 1748, and Fresnel independently conceived it again in 1819 for lighthouses. The first Fresnel lens for a lighthouse was lit on 25 July 1823 at the Cordouan lighthouse in France, and it is said to have been visible from more than 32 kilometers away.

The Lighthouse of Alexandria is counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. It is said to have been begun by Ptolemy I after 305 BCE and finished by Ptolemy II, and its completion is placed between 284 and 246 BCE. Apart from its height being more than 100 meters, estimates differ from source to source. It was brought down by earthquakes in 796, 951, and 956, and after the earthquake of 1303 only the base remained; in 1480 the Qaitbay Citadel was built on the site. How the fire at the top worked is not known in detail.

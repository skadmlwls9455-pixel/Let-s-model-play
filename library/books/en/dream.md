REM sleep became established as a stage of sleep when Aserinsky and Kleitman published it in the journal Science in 1953, and its link with dreaming became known at that time. REM stands for rapid eye movement. Adults spend 20 to 25 percent of a night's sleep, roughly 90 to 120 minutes, in REM sleep, usually divided into four or five episodes. The first REM sleep comes about 70 minutes after falling asleep and grows longer toward morning. The eye movements also occur in people who are blind from birth, so they are thought not to be movements directly tied to seeing something in a dream.

Dreams occur most often in REM sleep but not only there. Short, less vivid dreams are also reported in non-REM sleep, especially in the light stage 2. There is an estimate that about 80 percent of people woken during REM sleep report something of a dream.

The reason dreams are forgotten has not yet been settled into a single answer. What is known is that a dream is generally remembered if the person wakes in the middle of it, and otherwise it is forgotten unless it is especially intense or strange. There is a report that adults remember a dream about twice a week on average, and that this can increase with a habit of recording them. States similar to REM sleep appear in both land mammals and birds.

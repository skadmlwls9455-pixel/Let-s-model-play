The speed of light in a vacuum is 299,792,458 meters per second. Since 1983 the meter has been defined on the basis of this speed, so this value is a fixed value rather than a measurement result. Dividing the average distance between the Sun and the Earth by this speed gives about 8 minutes 19 seconds. The distance differs when the Earth is nearer to or farther from the Sun, so the actual figure ranges from a little over 8 minutes 10 seconds to 8 minutes 27 seconds. Therefore the sunlight that reaches the eye now is light that left the surface of the Sun about 8 minutes ago.

The first person to estimate numerically that the speed of light is finite was Ole Rømer, who in 1676 based his estimate on discrepancies in the timing of the orbits of Jupiter's moon Io. He calculated that light takes about 22 minutes to cross the diameter of the Earth's orbit.

Separate from the 8 minutes after leaving the Sun's surface, it takes much longer for energy to travel from the Sun's center to its surface. A photon produced at the center advances in a staggering path, being absorbed by the surrounding matter and re-emitted countless times. This time is estimated at between 10,000 and 170,000 years, and the exact value has not yet been settled.

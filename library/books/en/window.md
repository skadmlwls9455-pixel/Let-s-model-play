In England and Wales the window tax was introduced in 1696 under William III. A tax that directly probed income met strong resistance, and the number of windows in a house could be counted from the outside. At first each house paid 2 shillings, and paid more if it had over ten windows. A house with 10 to 20 windows paid 4 shillings, and one with over twenty paid 8 shillings. It was revised several times after that, and in 1709 the highest rate was applied to houses with thirty or more windows. The threshold number of windows on which tax was charged changed from ten to seven in 1766 and to eight in 1825. People who were exempt from parish taxes because of poverty were exempt from this tax too.

In Scotland it existed from 1748, in Ireland from 1799, and in France it was introduced in 1798 and lasted until 1926. The British window tax was abolished on 24 July 1851, and a tax on inhabited houses took its place. Critics said it taxed light and air. Adam Smith held that it could be counted from the outside and so was less intrusive.

The story that some houses had windows bricked up to avoid the tax is widely known. However, it cannot be said that every blocked window was due to the tax. Architecture in which false windows were deliberately made to balance the front of a building has existed since the Middle Ages, and in some cases windows were blocked because of the position of a staircase or a fireplace. So the reason for each blocked window can be known only with the records of that house.

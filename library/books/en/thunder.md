The air along the path of a lightning bolt becomes extremely hot in an instant. The encyclopedia gives the temperature of a lightning channel as about 20,000 kelvins (the U.S. National Weather Service says it can rise as high as 50,000 degrees Fahrenheit). The heated air expands explosively, and the shock wave is heard as the sound of thunder. Before the 20th century there was also an explanation that the sound arises when a vacuum collapses, but it is now explained by thermal expansion.

Light arrives practically at once, and sound arrives at about 343 meters per second. This speed is the value in dry air at 20 degrees Celsius. So by counting the time from the flash to the thunder, the distance can be estimated. Sound travels about 1 kilometer in 3 seconds. Counted in miles, it is 1 mile in 5 seconds. If it took 15 seconds, the strike is 3 miles away, that is, nearly 5 kilometers. It is a rough calculation, so there is some error.

Thunder is not a single "crack" but a long "rumble" because a lightning channel is long, stretching over several kilometers. Sound from the near part of the channel arrives first, and sound from the far part follows, so the sound continues. Close lightning tends to be heard as a sharp sound, and distant lightning as a low rumble. Thunder is usually said to be audible up to about 10 miles, that is, 16 kilometers.

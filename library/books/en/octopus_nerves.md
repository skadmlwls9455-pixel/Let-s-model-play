Chemical signals in the skin play a part in reducing how often an octopus's arms stick to one another. In the 2014 experiments by Nesher and colleagues, the suckers of an arm detached from the body did not attach to octopus skin. In the experiments, this response appeared with the skin of the same individual and with the skin of other octopuses alike. The ability to tell skin apart and the ability to tell individual identity apart are not the same thing.

On surfaces coated with skin extract, the force with which the suckers gripped was also reduced. The researchers interpreted this to mean that components of the skin suppress the suckers' attachment response. That the response remains in an arm detached from the body is evidence that this mechanism operates at the level of the arm's peripheral nervous system.

Living octopuses did sometimes grasp detached arms. The researchers interpreted this to mean that the central nervous system can override the peripheral inhibitory response. What this study explained is a mechanism that reduces unwanted attachment. It does not mean that the principle preventing every tangling of the arms has been fully worked out.

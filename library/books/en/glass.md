The main ingredient of glass is silicon dioxide, the chief component of sand. Pure silicon dioxide melts only at about 1723 degrees and is hard to work, so glass usually has sodium carbonate (soda) mixed in to lower the melting temperature. That makes it soluble in water, so lime and other materials are added to balance hardness and durability. Common soda-lime glass is 70 to 74 percent silicon by weight.

In a crystal the atoms are arranged in a regular pattern, so the directions of weakness are fixed. Glass is an amorphous solid with no such periodic arrangement, so when it breaks there are no predetermined cleavage planes. For this reason it often breaks with curved surfaces marked by shell-like ripples (conchoidal fracture). However, a crystalline material without cleavage planes can also break this way, so this shape alone cannot prove that a material is amorphous.

Sources differ on when glassmaking began. Some accounts push it back to before about 3600 BCE, while others say the oldest glass objects are beads from the middle of the 3rd millennium BCE. Steady production is said to have been established in Mesopotamia around 1600 BCE and in Egypt around 1500 BCE.

For a long time it was said that the unevenness of old window glass was caused by the glass flowing downward over hundreds of years. It is now held that hardened glass does not flow in this way, and that panes made by the old methods were uneven to begin with. A 2017 study of medieval glass in Westminster Abbey estimated that any flow would amount to about 1 nanometer per billion years. Crown glass was made by spinning an inflated ball of glass and spreading it into a disc, and it was thin at the edge and thick in the middle. Float glass, which is perfectly flat like sheet glass, became widespread only in the 1960s.

Hermann Ebbinghaus experimented on himself from 1880 to 1885. He memorized meaningless syllables such as "WID" and "ZOF," tested himself again at varied time intervals, and drew a graph of how memory declines as time passes. The results were published in his 1885 book Über das Gedächtnis (On Memory). He said that the speed of forgetting varies depending on whether the memorized content has meaning and on bodily conditions such as stress or sleep. In 2015, Murre and Dros repeated the same experiment on one person and obtained a similar curve.

That sleep helps memory is an old observation. In 1924, Jenkins and Dallenbach reported that, after the same amount of time, those who had slept remembered more than those who had stayed awake. The explanation widely cited today is the active systems consolidation hypothesis. It holds that the hippocampus, which learns new information quickly, holds it temporarily, and during slow-wave sleep repeats the same activity and transfers it to the neocortex, which takes charge of long-term storage. In the hippocampus of rats, neural activity resembling the order of experiences before sleep is observed to be repeated.

However, some parts are not certain. A review that examined the relationship between sleep spindles and memory across 53 studies found no clear relationship. There are reports that the link between sleep and memory is weaker in older people.

Snow crystals have six branches because the crystal structure of ice is hexagonal. Branches grow from the six corners of a crystal that began as a small hexagon, and since the six branches pass through nearly the same air, they grow into similar shapes. However, the shape in which a crystal grows varies with temperature and humidity, into plates, columns, needles and dendrites. Around minus 2 degrees Celsius, triangular crystals are rarely observed, and twelve-branched crystals are reported very occasionally.

A perfect six-branched star is rarer than one might think. Most snow particles that actually fall are irregular in shape, and one account says that those showing ideal symmetry make up less than 0.1 percent of the total. Wilson Bentley's photographs, taken through a microscope from 1885, showed that the shapes are extremely varied, and later research confirmed that simple symmetry is rare even in his photographs.

Two things overlap to make a snowy day quiet. Freshly fallen snow is a porous material and is explained to absorb sound well. In a 2020 experiment (90 kHz ultrasound), the reflection coefficient of freshly fallen snow at normal incidence was below 0.1, and after only one day the reflection increased markedly because of sintering, in which the snow grains fuse to one another. When snow melts and refreezes and the surface becomes hard, it is known to reflect sound more. In addition, there is the effect of fewer people and cars being out on a snowy day, so it is hard to separate out how much of the quiet is due to acoustics.

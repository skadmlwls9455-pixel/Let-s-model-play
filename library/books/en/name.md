Storms are given names so that forecasts and warnings do not become confused when there are several storms over the same ocean. In the past they were called by place names, by objects, or by the names of saints' feast days. The person usually counted as the first to use personal names is the Queensland meteorologist Clement Wragge, who did so from 1887 to 1907. The practice lapsed for a time and was revived in the western Pacific near the end of the Second World War.

A name is given only once a storm reaches a certain strength. In the North Atlantic the criterion is a sustained wind speed of 34 knots, and the criterion differs from ocean to ocean. Tropical depressions before that point are handled without names. In the Atlantic, the United States National Hurricane Center rotates through six lists of 21 names each. Names beginning with Q, U, X, Y and Z are left out, and men's and women's names alternate. If a year runs short of names, a supplementary list is used.

In the western Pacific, the Japan Meteorological Agency assigns the international names. The 14 members of the Typhoon Committee each contributed ten names in 1998, and the resulting 140 names are arranged in five sets. A typhoon that causes great damage can have its name retired and replaced with a new one at a member's request. A new name can be rejected because of its spelling or pronunciation, its similarity to other names, or its length. Separately from this, the Philippines uses its own list of names for its own waters.

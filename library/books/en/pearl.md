The substance that makes up a pearl is nacre. The mantle of the mollusk secretes calcium carbonate, mainly aragonite, and a protein called conchiolin binds it together, so that it is deposited in concentric layers. A single natural pearl is said to take several years to form.

It is commonly said that a pearl forms when a grain of sand gets in, but the source explains that this is rare. The actual irritant is often a fragment of organic matter, a parasite, or mantle tissue displaced by an injury, and it enters when the shell is opened for feeding or breathing. The main course is the same: the foreign body is wrapped in nacre.

Natural pearls in wild mollusks are so rare that hundreds may have to be opened to find one. Their shapes vary, and perfectly round ones are comparatively rare even among them.

Most pearls on the market now are cultured. The culturing method was not first devised by Mikimoto. A method developed in Australia by the British biologist William Saville-Kent was brought to Japan by Nishikawa Tokichi and Mise Tatsuhei, and Nishikawa received a patent in 1916. He was Mikimoto's son-in-law, and Mikimoto used the technique. Akoya pearls are known to be harvested after about 1 year, South Sea and Tahitian pearls after 2 to 4 years, and freshwater pearls after 2 to 7 years.

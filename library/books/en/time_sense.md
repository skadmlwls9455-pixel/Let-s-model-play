The time felt while waiting and the time recalled afterward can be judged differently. "How fast time passed" and "how many minutes went by" are also not the same judgment. Boredom is associated with the feeling that time flows slowly, but a long wait is not always left as a short memory.

In experiments with people, conditions are divided between one in which participants are told in advance that they will judge time and one in which they are first asked how much time passed after it has ended. In the former, attention directed to time, and in the latter, the memory cues formed in the meantime, are important explanatory factors.

There are also studies in which intervals where the way of handling a task changed were remembered as longer. On the basis of such results, it can be explained that a waiting time with little change may be judged short because there are fewer cues available at recall. This is an explanation extended from experimental results to daily life, and it is not a law that applies to every wait.

The flow of time felt in the present and the length of time reconstructed later are affected differently by attention, boredom, and the conditions under which memory is retrieved.

An echo is sound that strikes a wall or a mountain slope and comes back. The human ear cannot hear the original sound and the returning sound as separate if the gap between them is shorter than about 0.1 second. If the speed of sound in air is taken as a little over 340 meters per second, sound travels about 34 meters in 0.1 second. Since it must go and return, a wall about half that distance, 17 meters, is needed. Encyclopedias give 17.2 meters. Because the speed of sound varies with temperature, 17 meters is an approximate value. Also, 0.1 second is an average value, so it may differ slightly from person to person and from one kind of sound to another.

Many bats grasp their surroundings by the echoes of sounds they make themselves. By measuring the time between sending out a sound and its return, they can know the distance to an object. Bat calls are reported in a range of roughly 11 kilohertz to 212 kilohertz, and most lie above the limit of what humans can hear. Not all bats use this method. Some fruit bats use tongue clicks or wing sounds, and their precision is said to be lower than that of the method of making sound with the larynx.

In the 18th century, Spallanzani held that bats fly in the dark by a sense other than sight. In 1798, Jurine concluded that it was hearing. The term echolocation was coined by Donald Griffin.

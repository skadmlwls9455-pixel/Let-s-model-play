Tides are caused mainly by the gravity of the Moon and the Sun. The Moon's pull is slightly stronger on the side of the Earth facing the Moon and slightly weaker on the opposite side. This difference has the effect of stretching the Earth lengthwise toward the Moon, and because water can flow, it bulges in two places, on the near side and on the far side. As the Earth rotates through these bulges, high tide comes twice a day in most places.

It takes about 24 hours 50 minutes for the Moon to return to the same place in the sky, so the interval from one high tide to the next is not 12 hours but about 12 hours 25 minutes. For that reason the time of high tide gets later each day. The Sun's influence is about 46 percent of the Moon's. When the Sun, Moon, and Earth line up, as at the new moon and the full moon, the difference in water height is large, which is a spring tide, and at the first and last quarters it is small, which is a neap tide. The difference also becomes larger when the Moon is close to the Earth.

That there are two tides a day varies from place to place. There are places with two a day, places with one, and places where the two cycles are mixed unevenly. The shape of the ocean basins, the depth of the sea, the coastline, friction in shallow seas, and the effect of the Earth's rotation all combine, so real tides differ greatly from the simple picture. The large tidal range of the Bay of Fundy in Canada is also explained by the shape and depth of the bay.

Newton's equilibrium theory assumes an Earth with no land and no friction, so it explains the twice-daily swelling, but it cannot tell the timing and height at a particular coast. Such values are obtained from observation and harmonic analysis.

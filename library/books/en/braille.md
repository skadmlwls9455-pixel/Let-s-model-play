Louis Braille was born on 4 January 1809 in Coupvray, France. Around the age of three, while playing in his father's leather workshop, he injured an eye with an awl. The wound became infected and the infection spread to the other eye, and by about the age of five he was completely blind.

The basis of braille was a code called night writing, created by the soldier Charles Barbier. It used up to twelve raised dots to represent thirty-six sounds, so that orders could be read silently in the dark. Braille learned of it in 1821 and, drawing on it, devised a simpler six-dot system. It is known that he created the system in 1824, when he was fifteen. He presented it to his colleagues in 1825 and published it in a book in 1829.

Each of the six positions of dots can be either present or absent, which gives 64 patterns in all. Among these, the cell with no dots at all is used as the space between words, so 63 patterns are used as letters. Braille died in Paris on 6 January 1852, and the French school (institute) for the blind adopted this method two years later, in 1854.

The wooden locks of ancient Egypt consisted of a wooden post fixed to the door and a bolt that slid horizontally inside it. The bolt had holes drilled from top to bottom with pins inside them, and while the pins straddled the bolt, it could not move. Inserting a wooden key and pushing the pins up to the right height released the bolt. This principle is said to go back to Egypt around 2000 BCE, but sources are cautious about the exact date. It had the weakness that the lock also opened if the pins were pushed up too high.

The cylinder locks common today have the same basic structure. Springs press the upper pins down, and when the right key is inserted, each stack of pins rises according to the height of the key's teeth, so that the gaps between the pins line up along the boundary between the rotating plug and the body. Then the plug turns. If the key is wrong, the pins cross the boundary and block it.

The modern type was made in 1848 by Linus Yale Sr., and in 1861 his son Yale Jr. patented a flat key with teeth and a configuration of pins of different lengths. Earlier, in 1805, the American physician Abraham Stansbury received an early patent in England for a two-way design in which the lock does not open if the pins are raised too far.

The Romans made locks that used metal keys and wards, and the wealthy are said to have worn keys like rings. A warded lock opens only when the grooves of the key match the obstacles inside, but its security is weak, and in many cases a single skeleton key can open several.

In 2015, UCLA researchers isolated the stem cells and progenitor cells that form the basis of immune cells from human bone marrow and thymus and read their RNA in its entirety. These cells make up less than 1% of the cells in the bone marrow and thymus. Among them, the researchers found more than 3,000 previously unknown genes for long non-coding RNA. These RNAs do not make proteins, but they were switched on in different combinations during the process in which cells diverge, with some becoming B cells and others becoming T cells. The study was published in Nature Immunology.

The 3,000 is the number of entries on the list that was found, and what each one does is still mostly unknown. The nickname "junk DNA" meant that the function was unknown, not that it was useless.

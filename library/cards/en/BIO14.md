In 2015, a team at the Weizmann Institute in Israel secretly filmed people greeting each other and counted how often they brought their own hands close to their faces after a handshake. After shaking hands with someone of the same sex, the behavior of bringing the right hand that had done the shaking close to the nose increased more than twofold compared with before the handshake. After shaking hands with someone of the opposite sex, sniffing of the left hand, which had not shaken, increased instead. When measured with gloves, chemical compounds from the other person's skin were found to have actually transferred onto the hand that shook. The team interpreted this as evidence that humans, too, engage in behavior that collects chemical signals.

The experiment filmed several hundred people and counted how often they brought a hand close to the face. It did not establish what people learn from that smell.

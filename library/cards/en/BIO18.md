The wombat is the only known animal that produces nearly cube-shaped droppings. The shape is set inside the intestine, not by a square anus. According to a study published in Soft Matter in 2021 by teams from the Georgia Institute of Technology in the United States and the University of Tasmania in Australia, the corners form in the last 17% of the roughly 10 m long intestine. In the wall of this section, bands about twice as thick and about four times as stiff as the rest alternate around the circumference, and when the intestine contracts, the stiff and soft parts move at different speeds and form the corners. This was confirmed by dissection, tissue examination, tensile testing and a mathematical model. The wombat's intestine draws out a lot of water, so the droppings become firm and the shape that has been set remains.

The explanation that cube-shaped droppings do not roll away and so are well suited to being stacked on rocks to mark territory is a hypothesis. What the study established is the mechanics by which the shape forms.
